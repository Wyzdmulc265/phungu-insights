How to Pick a Company to Buy start here

A practical order of operations using this site's own tools. It is not a formula for guaranteed profit — it is a disciplined way to narrow 17 listings down to a decision you can defend.

  • 1. Decide what you want. Income (dividends), growth (price appreciation), or stability? On the MSE, income is historically the most reliable of the three.
  • 2. Start wide, then narrow. Open the Outlook tab and scan the verdict chips. Buy · Undervalued names (rising, still cheap) are the classic hunting ground. Buy · Overvalued means the trend is real but you are paying premium prices. Sell · Undervalued means cheap and still falling — cheapness alone is not a reason.
  • 3. Open the company page. Check the reasons under the verdict, then the fundamentals: a P/E far above its sector needs a story; a dividend yield needs a payout the company actually sustains; a P/B over 1 means you are paying for more than book value.
  • 4. Read the reasons, not just the label. "Strong 3M momentum" fades; "dividend yield above market" is cash in hand. Know which kind of gain you are buying.
  • 5. Check you can actually trade it. Some MSE listings are thin — days pass with almost no volume. Look at the volume bars on the company page before assuming you can buy or exit at the displayed price.
  • 6. Diversify across sectors. Banks dominate this market's listings. Two bank tickers is one bet, not two.
  • 7. Decide your exit before you enter. A price you would sell at, a yield drop you would act on, or a thesis that would change your mind. Write it down.
  • 8. Re-check after each weekly model run. Verdicts move. Revisit your reasons, not just your holdings.

This is general education about method, not a recommendation of any specific security. Nothing on this site is investment advice.

Price-to-Earnings (P/E)

What you pay per kwacha of yearly profit. A P/E of 10 means the share price equals ten years of current earnings.

  • Low P/E → the market is paying little for each kwacha of profit (cheap, or profits expected to fall)
  • High P/E → the market is paying a lot (expensive, or growth expected)
  • Loss-making companies have no meaningful P/E — the model leaves them out of that factor instead of inventing a number

On a 17-stock exchange, P/E only means something relative to the other listed companies, which is exactly how the model uses it.

Dividend Yield

Cash returned per year as a percentage of the share price. A 5% yield on a 10,000 MK share pays 500 MK a year, regardless of what the price does.

  • High yield can mean generous payouts — or a price that has fallen
  • Yields move with price: same dividend, lower price, higher yield

Price-to-Book (P/B) new

The share price versus the company's accounting net worth (book value = assets minus debts on the balance sheet). A P/B of 0.6 means you buy 1 kwacha of net assets for 60 tambala.

  • P/B under 1 → trading below book value (e.g. MPICO, ICON historically) — either neglected or its assets earn poorly
  • P/B well over 1 → the market pays for something the balance sheet doesn't show: brands, licences, customer relationships
  • Malawian banks trade at high P/B because their book grows with retained profits

Unlike a DCF, P/B needs no forecasting — just the balance sheet the exchange already publishes daily. That is why the model uses it.

How the Value Layer Combines Them

Each factor is converted to a z-score — how far above or below the market average it sits, measured in standard deviations. Then they are weighted:

value score = 0.40 × z(P/E, low=cheap) + 0.30 × z(dividend yield) + 0.30 × z(P/B, low=cheap)

  • Positive score → cheaper than the average listed company
  • Negative score → more expensive than average
  • If a factor is missing for a stock, the remaining weights rescale so the score stays honest

What a Z-Score Is

"z = +1.5" simply means "1.5 standard deviations above the market average." About two-thirds of stocks always sit between −1 and +1 — so anything beyond ±0.5 is worth a look, and beyond ±1 is genuinely unusual.

Z-scores let the model compare kwacha prices, percentages and ratios on one honest scale, without pretending to know fair value in absolute terms.

Technical Layer (Momentum)

  • 3-month return — where the price has gone recently (50%)
  • 1-month return — the fresher trend (30%)
  • Price vs 20-day average — trading above or below its recent mean (20%)

The composite is z-scored the same way as the value layer. Strong positive → Buy territory; strong negative → Sell territory; the middle → Hold.

News Sentiment — the Tiebreaker

Each week, recent company announcements and Malawi market news are scored from −1 (bearish) to +1 (bullish) per stock. Sentiment never overrides a strong signal — it only nudges borderline cases one notch. A stock with middling momentum but positive news becomes Buy; middling momentum with bad news becomes Sell.

Reading the Verdict Chips

Every company page and the Outlook tab show two chips:

BUY HOLD SELL   the stance, from momentum + sentiment

UNDERVALUED FAIRLY VALUED OVERVALUED   the valuation call, from the value layer

  • Buy · Undervalued — rising and still cheap (the combination everyone hunts)
  • Buy · Overvalued — momentum is real but you pay premium prices for it
  • Sell · Undervalued — cheap and falling; value needs a trigger to turn

Below each verdict are up to three reasons — the specific factors that drove it.

Why No DCF or Price Targets

A discounted cash-flow model needs reliable forecasts of growth and discount rates. The MSE's short, irregular history would force us to invent those inputs — precise-looking arithmetic built on guesses. The z-score approach only claims what the data supports: cheaper or dearer, stronger or weaker, than the rest of this market.

Limits You Should Know

  • Value factors come from official PDF reports; P/B and P/E refresh when new reports are filed, not every day
  • Thin trading means one large trade can move a "momentum" reading
  • The verdict thresholds are starting points — they will be calibrated against observed outcomes over time
